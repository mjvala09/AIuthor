# Guide to Investing and Retirement Accounts

## The Power of Compound Interest
Compound interest is the interest you earn on interest. It is the primary engine of long-term wealth building.
- **Formula**: $A = P(1 + r/n)^{nt}$
  - $A$ = total future amount
  - $P$ = principal investment
  - $r$ = annual interest rate
  - $n$ = number of times interest is compounded per year
  - $t$ = number of years
- **The Rule of 72**: A quick way to estimate how long it will take for your money to double at a given annual interest rate. Divide 72 by your interest rate. For example, at an 8% return rate, your money will double in $72 / 8 = 9$ years.
- **Time Horizon**: Starting early is critical. An individual who saves $300/month from age 20 to 30 and stops will end up with more money at age 65 than someone who saves $300/month from age 30 to 65, assuming a constant 8% annual return, due to the extra decade of compounding.

## Asset Classes

### 1. Stocks (Equities)
Buying a stock means purchasing a small share of ownership in a public company. Stocks offer high growth potential but come with high volatility and risk. Over the long term (100+ years), the U.S. stock market (S&P 500) has returned an average of approximately **10% annually** (before adjusting for inflation).

### 2. Bonds (Fixed Income)
A bond is a loan you make to a corporation or government in exchange for regular interest payments (coupons) and the return of the principal when the bond matures. Bonds are generally safer than stocks but offer lower returns.

### 3. Mutual Funds and ETFs (Exchange-Traded Funds)
Instead of buying individual stocks, you can invest in mutual funds or ETFs, which bundle hundreds of stocks or bonds together.
- **Index Funds**: A passive type of mutual fund or ETF that tracks a market index (like the S&P 500 or the Total Stock Market index). They have very low fees (expense ratios under 0.05% per year) and consistently outperform active mutual funds over long periods.

## Retirement Accounts (US-Specific)

### 1. 401(k) / 403(b)
Employer-sponsored retirement plans.
- **Employer Match**: Many employers offer matching contributions (e.g., if you contribute 5% of your salary, they will match it with another 5%). Financial advisors call this "free money" and recommend contributing at least enough to get the full match.
- **Traditional vs Roth**:
  - **Traditional 401(k)**: Pre-tax contributions. This lowers your taxable income today, but you pay regular income taxes when you withdraw the money in retirement.
  - **Roth 401(k)**: Post-tax contributions. You pay taxes on the money now, but withdrawals in retirement are 100% tax-free.

### 2. IRAs (Individual Retirement Accounts)
Self-directed accounts opened at brokerages (like Fidelity, Vanguard, Schwab).
- **Traditional IRA**: Contributions may be tax-deductible depending on your income and whether you have a workplace retirement plan. Growth is tax-deferred; tax is paid upon withdrawal.
- **Roth IRA**: Contributions are made with after-tax money. The money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals after age 59.5 are tax-free.

## Asset Allocation and Diversification
Diversification is the practice of spreading investments across different asset classes (stocks, bonds, real estate) and sectors to minimize risk.
- **Asset Allocation**: The mix of stocks and bonds in a portfolio. Younger investors with a long time horizon should hold a higher percentage of stocks (e.g., 90% stocks, 10% bonds) for growth, while older investors closer to retirement should hold more bonds (e.g., 60% stocks, 40% bonds) to protect their capital.
